# Figure captions (draft, for the LaTeX report)

## Figure 1, option 1 (`fig1_option1_bars`)
**Even on the main summer holiday, as many people sleep in inland towns and villages as on the coast.**
Each bar is one night of 2024. It shows the people who slept at least 100 km from their home zone, split by where they slept: inland towns and villages (terracotta), inland cities over 100,000 people (grey) and coastal zones (blue). The numbers inside the bars and on the right are millions of people. On the night of 14–15 August, 5.5 times as many people slept far from home as on an ordinary weekday. Coastal stays grew 8.6-fold and stays in inland towns and villages 5.9-fold, so the two are almost level (2.2 and 2.1 million). On the family holidays (All Saints and Christmas Eve), inland towns and villages receive about twice as many people as the coast. With a 50 or 200 km threshold instead of 100 km, the inland share moves by at most 6.5 points across the five nights (5.0 points in August). Data: MITMA overnight-stay estimates from mobile phones; 2,435 mainland zones; a zone is coastal if it touches the sea.

## Figure 1, option 2 (`fig1_option2_calendar`)
**Where Spaniards sleep when they are far from home, night by night.**
**a**, People sleeping at least 100 km from their home zone on every night of 2024 with data, in inland towns and villages (terracotta) and on the coast (blue). The shading shows which of the two received more people that night. Gaps are months without data. Each night is dated by the evening it starts, and dashed lines mark the five nights studied. Outside August, inland towns and villages receive more people than the coast on 149 of the 150 nights with data (the exception is 31 July), with peaks every weekend and on long weekends. In August the coast is slightly ahead on 29 of 30 nights, but the two lines move together. **b**, The five nights studied, in millions of people, now also showing inland cities over 100,000 people (grey). On 14–15 August, 5.5 times as many people slept far from home as on an ordinary weekday, and inland towns and villages (2.1 million) almost match the coast (2.2 million). Data: MITMA overnight-stay estimates from mobile phones; 2,435 mainland zones; a zone is coastal if it touches the sea.

## Figure 2 (`fig2_senders_receivers`)
**On the summer holiday, the network splits into zones that send people and zones that receive them.**
**a, b**, Each point is one of the 2,435 zones (network with links of at least 10 people). The horizontal axis counts how many zones its residents sleep in (out-degree) and the vertical axis how many zones send residents to sleep in it (in-degree); both axes are logarithmic, and points are jittered slightly so that zones with equal counts do not overlap. On an ordinary weekday (a), zones that send people to many places also receive people from many places, so the points lie along the diagonal (Spearman correlation 0.69). On 14–15 August (b) this link almost disappears (0.09). Cities (black) move below the diagonal: Madrid's residents sleep in 2,203 zones, but people from only 995 zones sleep in Madrid. Coastal towns (blue) move above it. Of the 50 zones that receive people from most places, 80% are coastal in August, against 36% in February. **c**, Degree assortativity r measures whether well-connected zones link to each other (r > 0) or to poorly connected ones (r < 0). The grey bar is the range of r reached by rewiring the network towards maximal assortativity and maximal disassortativity while keeping every degree and a single connected component (Zhou & Mondragón 2007; a heuristic search, so the true limits may lie slightly further out). The tick is the mean of 50 degree-preserving random rewirings and the dot is the real network. In August the possible range moves and widens (from −0.22…−0.09 to −0.42…−0.11), and the real network stays close to where random rewiring puts it (−0.214 against −0.217); rewiring reproduces about 93 % of the change in r from February. Most of the stronger mixing of hubs with small zones in August therefore follows from the new sender–receiver degrees; the small remaining gap is measurable (about 6 standard deviations of the rewired networks) but does not change the picture.

## Figure 3 (`fig3_roots_generation`)
**On the summer holiday, home provinces pull harder inland, and the extra pull comes from residents who arrived before 1981 and from the village-born.**
For each city, the census gives how many residents were born in each province. A gravity model (Poisson, with city fixed effects, origin-province × destination-province fixed effects, distance and city income × destination controls) estimates how strongly the number of residents from a province predicts how many of the city's residents sleep there. The horizontal axis is how much stronger that pull is on 14–15 August than on 13–14 February; +0.1 means about 7% more travellers to a province for each doubling of the group from it. Bars are 95% intervals from a wild-cluster bootstrap by province (44 provinces). **a**, Left: trips to inland destinations; right: trips to coastal destinations. Residents who arrived in their current province before 1981 (mostly the generation of the rural exodus) and residents born in a village of 2,000 people or fewer pull more people to their home provinces in August, but only to inland destinations (terracotta, left); towards the coast (blue, right) no change can be detected. Recent migrants show the opposite. **b**, Age or generation? Residents aged 65+ who arrived after 1981 show no detectable extra pull (−0.006, 95 % interval −0.04 to 0.02); age alone does not produce the effect. **c**, The test repeated on every pair of one summer night (the nine nights ending 10–18 August) and one ordinary weekday night in February (13 nights, 117 pairs; the pairs share nights, so they are not independent tests): the sign is the same in all 117. The size of the effect depends on how empty city–province cells are treated; the sign does not (Supplementary).


## Figure 4 (`fig4_villages`)
**On holiday nights, the emptied villages stop sending people away and start receiving them.**
**a**, Net balance of each of the 2,435 zones: people from elsewhere who sleep in the zone (in-strength) minus residents of the zone who sleep elsewhere (out-strength), divided by their sum. White zones send more people than they receive; shaded zones receive more than they send, darker meaning a larger surplus. On an ordinary weekday, the emptied interior (the northern plateau, Aragon, the Iberian mountains) is almost all white: its residents leave. On 14–15 August and on Christmas Eve the same interior is shaded, while the big cities become white holes (Madrid goes from +0.07 to −0.80 in August and −0.49 on Christmas Eve). **b**, Share of zones that receive more people than they send, by how much population the zone has lost since its historical peak (1900–1960) up to 2021, in fifths of about 475 zones (dashed grey line with open circles: ordinary weekday; black line with open squares: 14–15 August; black line with filled diamonds: Christmas Eve). Greys are used on purpose: in the other figures blue, terracotta and black mean coast, inland and cities. On an ordinary weekday, only 24% of the most emptied fifth receive more than they send; on 14–15 August 99%, and on Christmas Eve over 99%. The switch holds with zone size, zone aggregation, distance to the nearest city, coast and province held fixed, and in zones made of a single municipality. The mechanism differs by holiday: in August it is mostly residents of emptied zones staying at home while visitors match similar places; at Christmas, visitors per resident rise as well.

## Basic properties, section 3.2 (`fig_basic_properties`)
**On holiday the network gets denser, and its mixing is mostly what the degrees alone produce; on the summer night, so is most of its rich club.**
Three properties of the undirected network of three nights of 2024 (links of at least 10 people), in the layout of Zhou & Mondragón (2007, Fig. 2). Dashed grey line with open circles: ordinary weekday (13–14 February); grey line with filled triangles: All Saints (31 October–1 November); black line with open squares: summer holiday (14–15 August). Thin dotted lines: the same network after random rewiring that keeps every zone's degree (b: mean of 10 rewirings; c: mean of 50 rewirings that keep the network connected); where no dotted line is visible it lies under the real curve. **a**, Share of the 2,435 zones with degree at least *k* (complementary cumulative distribution); the dotted vertical line marks the maximum possible degree, all 2,434 other zones. A Clauset–Shalizi–Newman likelihood-ratio test cannot tell a power law from a log-normal on the ordinary and All Saints nights and favours the log-normal on the summer night (p < 0.05). **b**, Mean degree of a zone's neighbours, *k*<sub>nn</sub>, against its own degree (logarithmic bins with at least 5 zones). It falls with *k* on every night: well-connected zones link mostly to poorly connected ones (disassortative mixing). The rewired networks give almost the same curves. **c**, Rich-club connectivity φ(*r*), the share of possible links that exist among the *r* best-connected zones, against the normalised rank *r*/*N* (Zhou & Mondragón 2004). The best-connected zones are more tightly linked than in the rewired network on the ordinary and All Saints nights (top 50: φ = 0.80 against 0.66, and 0.91 against 0.81), but hardly on the summer night (0.96 against 0.92; Table 1). Sources: `results/b02_degree_ccdf.parquet`, `results/b03_knn.csv`, `results/b02_rich_club_zhou.csv`, `results/b02_powerlaw.csv`.

## BOSAM, appendix (`fig_bosam`), cited in section 4.1
**The three nights have the same overall shape: the summer network looks like the ordinary one, denser.**
Bitmap of the sorted adjacency matrix (BOSAM; Guo, Chen & Zhou 2008) of the undirected network of three nights of 2024 (links of at least 10 people). Zones are sorted by degree, least connected first (ties: largest neighbour degree, then largest neighbour position); a dark cell at (*i*, *j*) means that zones *i* and *j* are linked. **a–c**, The whole matrix. A printed pixel cannot hold one of the 2,435 × 2,435 cells, so each grey level is the share of linked pairs in a block of 25 × 25 zones, on one logarithmic scale shared by the three nights. On every night the dark band along the top and the right edge shows the same pattern: zones of every degree link mainly to a few best-connected zones, Madrid last in the order (disassortative mixing). The pattern looks the same on holiday nights; the visible change is density (3.4 % of pairs linked in summer against 0.9 % on the ordinary night). The BOSAM ignores the direction of links, so the split into sending and receiving zones (Figure 2) does not show here. **d–f**, The 121 best-connected zones (top 5 %, the outlined square in a–c), one cell per pair. They are linked in 44 % of pairs on the ordinary night, 59 % at All Saints and 78 % on the summer night; random rewiring that keeps every degree gives 38 %, 55 % and 76 % (Table A1), so on the summer night almost all of this follows from the degrees. The strip below gives the kind of zone at each position: the top of the order is made of cities on every night. The self-similar envelope test of Guo, Chen & Zhou is not informative here: the largest-index neighbour of 62 % (ordinary), 82 % (All Saints) and 92 % (summer) of zones is Madrid (these are Madrid's degree shares), so most leaf envelopes end on Madrid's row (`results/b04_bosam_envelopes_summary.csv`). Sources: `results/b02_bosam_order_*__w10.csv`, `results/b02_bosam_edges_*__w10.parquet`.
